# Probabilistic Approaches
- **Core Definition:** The foundation paradigm in robotics that uses probability theory to explicitly model and manage uncertainty in both robot motion (actuation) and environment perception (sensing).
- **Real-World vs Simulation:**
	- **Simulation:** Idealized environment operate deterministically under perfect assumptions.
	- **Reality:** Physical robots continuously encounter noise, calibration errors, and unpredictable environment disturbances.
	- **Example:** Commanding a robot to move at 30 km/h may result in actual speeds of 30,31 or 32 km/h due to wheel slip, friction and motor noise.
- **Belief Representation:** Instead of relying on single, deterministic values, system states are represented as probability distributions to reflect varying degrees of confidence.
- **Robustness & Fault Tolerance:** Probabilistic models allow robots to degrade gracefully during partial failures, preventing complete system stops and enabling principled decision-making despite noisy or incomplete data.

# Joint and Conditional Probability 
- $P(X=x \text{ and }Y=y) = P(x,y)$ 
- if X and Y are **independent** then ([[Independent Event]])
	- $P(x,y) = P(x)P(y)$
- $P(x|y)$ is the probability of **X given Y** ([[Bayes Theorem]])
	- $P(x,y) = P(x|y)P(y)$ 
	- $P(x|y) = \dfrac{P(x,y)}{P(y)}$
- if X and Y are **independent** then ([[Independent Event]])
	- $P(x|y) = P(x)$

# Bayes Rule with Background Knowledge
$$P(x|y,z) = \dfrac{P(y|x,z)P(x|z)}{P(y|z)}$$

# Conditional Independence  [[Conditional Independence|#]]
Two variables are conditional independence if
$$P(x,y|z) = P(x|z)P(y|z)$$
- Equivalent to 
$$
\begin{align}
P(x|z)=P(x|z,y) \\
P(y|z)=P(y|z,x)
\end{align}
$$
- But this does not necessarily mean
$$P(x,y)=P(x)P(y)$$

**Question: Understanding $p(x_{t}|z_{1: t},y_{1: y})$**
A mobile robot moves through an environment containing three possible positions:
<center>A, B, C</center>
The robot does not know exactly where it is. It maintains a **belief** about its position.
at time $t-1$, its belief is:
$$\begin{align}
P(x_{t-1}=A)&=0.6 \\
P(x_{t-1}=B)&=0.3 \\
P(x_{t-1}=C)&=0.1
\end{align}$$
The robot receives information about its **motion/control**, represented by $y_t$, and performs a movement $u_t$.
After moving, its sensor observes a door:
$$ z_{t} = \text{door detected}$$
The sensor model is:
$$\begin{align}
P(z_{t}=door|x_{t}=A)=0.1\\
P(z_{t}=door|x_{t}=B)=0.9\\
P(z_{t}=door|x_{t}=C)=0.2
\end{align}$$
Explains what
$$p(x_{t}|z_{1: t},y_{1: t})$$
means and how the robot uses its previous belief, motion information, and sensor measurement to estimate its current position.

---
# Recursive Bayes Filter 1
$$\begin{align}
bel(x_{t})&=p(x_{t}|z_{1:t},y_{1: t})\\
&=\eta \space p(z_{t}|x_{t},z_{1: t-1},u_{1: t})p(x_{t}|z_{1:t-1},y_{1: t})
\end{align}$$
Proof:
$$\begin{align}
p(x_{t}|z_{1: t},u_{1: t})&=p(x_{t}|z_{t},z_{1: t-1},u_{1: t})\\
&= \dfrac{p(z_{t}|x_{t},z_{1: t-1},u_{1: t})\space p(x_{t}|z_{1: t-1},u_{1: t})}{p(z_{t}|z_{1: t-1},u_{1: t})}\\
&=\eta \space p(z_{t}|x_{t},z_{1: t-1},u_{1: t})\space p(x_{t}|z_{1: t-1},u_{1: t})
\end{align}$$
hence, once we know the current position $x_{t}$, past states, past sensor reading, and past controls provide no additional information about the current measurement: $p(z_{t}|x_{t},z_{1: t-1},u_{1: t})=p(z_{t}|x_{t})$
$$
\begin{align}
&= \eta \space p(z_{t}|x_{t}) \space p(x_{t}|z_{1: t-1},u_{1: t}) \\
&= \eta \space p(z_{t|x_{t}}) \int p(x_{t}|x_{t-1},z_{1: t-1},u_{1: t}).p(x_{t-1}|z_{1: t-1},u_{1: t})dx_{t-1} \\
&=\eta \space p(z_{t}|x_{t}) \int p(x_{t}|x_{t-1},u_{t}).p(x_{t-1}|z_{1: t-1},u_{1: t})dx_{t-1} \space \text{(markov assumption)} \\
&=\eta \space p(z_{t}|x_{t}) \int p(x_{t}|x_{t-1},u_{t}).p(x_{t-1}|z_{1: t-1},u_{1: t-1})dx_{t-1} \\
&=\eta \space p(z_{t}|x_{t}) \int p(x_{t}|x_{t-1},u_{t}).bel(x_{t-1})dx_{t-1}
\end{align}
$$
# Prediction and Correction Step
- Bayes filter can be written as a two step process.
- **Prediction Step:**
$$\overline{bel}(x_{t})=\eta \space p(z_{t}|x_{t}).\overline{bel}(x_{t})$$
- **Correction Step:**
$$bel(x_{t})=\eta \space p(z_{t}|x_{t}).\overline{bel}(x_{t})$$
